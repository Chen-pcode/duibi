LMDAU-Net: An Effective Lightweight Multi-scale Deformation

Aggregation U-Net for Skin Lesion Segmentation

Jun Hu, Ming Liu, Jing Yang, Pengxiang Su, Sunil Prajapat, Dheeraj Kumar, Por Lip Yee and Dalong Wei

Abstract—Automatic skin lesion segmentation is a pivotal
problem in the medical domain and an indispensable component
in the computer-aided diagnosis program. Most convolutional
neural network-based segmentation algorithms have demon-
strated promising performance due to their ability to encode
detail and semantic features efﬁciently. However, they fail to
capture the long-range contextual information at the global
level. Therefore, researchers employ Transformer architecture
to address this issue. Unfortunately, these methods fail to learn
sufﬁcient pixel information at the local level. Motivated by
this, some researchers attempt to design a hybrid architecture
based on CNN and Transformer. However, the large number of
parameters and high computational cost make them challenging
to train and use. To alleviate these problems, we propose an
effective Lightweight Multi-scale Deformation Aggregation U-
Net (LMDAU-Net), which consists of a Lightweight Local-global
Learning Module (LLM) and an Adaptive Interactive Fusion
Module (AIF). Speciﬁcally, we utilize the two branches of the
proposed LLM to efﬁciently learn local ﬁne-grained and global
coarse-grained features that assist the model in capturing the
complementary feature representations. Moreover, we employ
the AIF to selectively learn semantic and detail features at
different scales, which can dynamically explore variable feature
cues. Extensive experiments on four skin benchmarks, including
ISIC2016, ISIC 2017, ISIC2018, and PH2, demonstrate that
LMDAU-Net achieves state-of-the-art performance in both qual-
itative and quantitative aspects. We have released our code on
https://github.com/Lm0611/LMDAU-Net.

Index Terms—Skin lesion segmentation, lightweight global-
local feature modeling, long-range contextual learning.

II. RELATED WORK

A. Medical image segmentation based on CNN

In the past decade, numerous CNN-based architectures
have been developed for medical image segmentation tasks
[21]–[24]. Google-Net [25] and Inception-Net [26]–[28] are
classical multi-branch frameworks for feature propagation,
demonstrating superior performance. Early architectures like
AlexNet [29] and VGGNet [30] revealed that stacking convo-
lutional and pooling layers enhances network depth to capture
richer feature information. ResNet established the residual
skip connections between backbone layers to address gradient
vanishing issues. The U-Net framework [31], with its sym-
metric encoder-decoder design, establishes an indispensable
role in medical image segmentation. Subsequent variants like
UNet++ [32] designed several nested dense skip connections
to bridge semantic disparities. While Attention U-Net [33]
integrated attention gates, allowing the algorithm to prioritize
the varying shapes and sizes of targets. DoubleU-Net [34]
combined dual U-Net cascades with Atrous Spatial Pyramid
Pooling (ASPP) to learn better small structure information.
DSNet [35] optimized parameter count and enhanced network
efﬁciency by introducing depth-wise separable convolutions
to their model. UNet3+ [36] incorporated deep supervision

with multi-scale skip connections, combining the masks of
the previous stage with current feature maps during training.

B. Image segmentation based on transformer

The restricted receptive ﬁelds of CNN-based models hinder
them from capturing long-range information. In contrast, the
Transformer-based model performs excellently in capturing
global information. For example, Vision Transformer (ViT)
adopts a pure Transformer architecture, achieving satisfac-
tory performance in image recognition. DeiT [37] addressed
the challenge of Transformers requiring vast datasets by in-
troducing a novel distillation-based training strategy. Swin
Transformer [38] introduced a novel hierarchical architecture
and self-attention mechanism based on a shifting window,
achieving satisfactory performance across recognition, detec-
tion, and segmentation tasks. TransUNet [39] introduced the
Transformer architecture into medical image segmentation and
showed powerful encoding capabilities. PVT [40] adopted the
pyramid structure of CNNs and integrated it into ViT, enabling
multi-scale predictions for dense tasks. DS-TransUNet [41]
to further capture richer information using multi-scale input
patches and dual Swin Transformer encoders.

C. Image segmentation based on mamba

Transformer-based models show better performance in cap-
turing global-related features. However, the quadratic compu-
tational complexity of transformer architectures limits their
use in dense prediction tasks, such as medical image seg-
mentation. Recently, state-space models (SSM) [13], [42]–[44]
have received attention due to their ability to encode long-
range dependency and maintain low computational complexity.
Among them, the Mamba model [14] has adopted a selective
state space mechanism, becoming a competitive alternative to
CNN and Transformer architectures. VM-UNet [45] was one
of the ﬁrst attempts to introduce Mamba-based model inte-
gration in medical imaging by embedding visual state space
(VSS) blocks [46] into U-Net architectures, which signiﬁcantly
balanced the efﬁciency and global context capture. VM-
UNetV2 [47] fused the multi-level features by combining the
VSS blocks and semantic-detail infusion modules to perform
better in multiple public medical image datasets. Efﬁcient at-
tention mechanisms also provide alternatives to standard self-
attention. For example, Performer employs random feature-
based attention approximation to achieve linear time and space
complexity [48], while Linformer reduces the computational
cost through low-rank projections of the attention matrix [49].
These methods provide efﬁcient global interaction modeling;
however, their attention computation relies on approximations
of the original attention mechanism. In contrast, Mamba
introduces input-dependent selective state space parameters
[14], enabling the model to selectively propagate or discard
information according to the input while maintaining linear
scaling with sequence length. Therefore, we adopt Mamba
as the global modeling branch in LLM to complement the
local feature extraction capability of CNNs. Although recent
works such as VM-UNet and VM-UNetV2 have explored hy-
brid CNN-Mamba architectures, our Lightweight Local-global

III. OUR APPROACH

In this section, the pipeline of our designed LMDAU-
Net is illustrated in Fig. 2ﬁgure.2. First, we introduce a
lightweight local-global learning module, which is crucial for
capturing sufﬁcient local and global features. Then, we show
that our adaptive interactive fusion module that beneﬁts from
selectively fusing closely related information from multi-scale
features. Finally, we elaborate on the loss function.

A. Lightweight Local-global Learning Module

The lightweight local-global learning module is a dual
branch architecture, as shown in Fig. 3ﬁgure.3(a), that uti-
lizes a CNN branch and an Mamba branch to synergistically
capture complementary local-global information. Speciﬁcally,

the input feature map has been split into two sub-maps of
the same size along the channel and fed into two branches.
The CNN branch consists of several convolutional layers to
encode local features from the sub-map, which can effectively
capture ﬁne-grained details crucial for delineating the intricate
boundaries of skin lesions.

Meanwhile, the Mamba branch has been proposed for
exploring long-range dependencies and integrating global con-
text, which addresses the limitations of CNNs in establishing
global interactions. Notably, we designed a novel Mamba-
based VM block and Multi-branch Extraction Block to capture
signiﬁcant global features. We integrate the VM block as the
fundamental component of LMDAU-Net. The VM module
(Fig. 3ﬁgure.3c) addresses the challenges of processing 2D
visual data via its selective 2D scanning (SS2D) mechanism
[46]. This approach decomposes input image patches along
four directional axes, producing corresponding independent
feature sequences. It overcomes the locality and directional
bias issues of traditional convolution in terms of spatial per-
ception, enabling the model to comprehensively understand the
interdependencies among pixels in the image, which is helpful
for extracting feature information. Each feature sequence was
input into SSM and merged to reconstruct the ﬁnal 2D spatial
representation. Formally, the SS2D operation for an input

C. Loss Functions

In this paper, our loss function consists of two parts. (1)
We utilize four prediction heads corresponding to the four
stages of the hierarchical encoders. The ﬁnal prediction map
is obtained via additive aggregation, which can be described
as:

Final prediction = i × p1 + j × p2 + k × p3 + l × p4 (6)

where p1, p2, p3, and p4 represent the feature maps from the
four prediction heads, while i, j, k, and l denote the weights
assigned to each prediction head. We set the values of i, j,
k, and l to 1.0 in all our experiments. The Final prediction
is fed into the sigmoid activation function to obtain the ﬁnal
binary segmentation mask.

(2) We calculate the loss for each prediction head to
supervise the learning of image segmentation:

loss = α×lossp1 +β ×lossp2 +γ ×lossp3 +ζ ×lossp4 (7)

where lossp1, lossp2, lossp3, and lossp4 are the loss of four
different prediction heads. α, β, γ, and ζ are the weights for
the loss of individual prediction heads. In our experiments, α,
β, γ, and ζ were all set to 1.0.

D. Auxiliary Edge Supervision

Although the proposed AIF module enhances multi-scale
feature interaction and boundary-aware feature learning, con-
ventional region-based supervision alone may not explicitly
constrain the predicted lesion contours. Therefore, we intro-
duce an auxiliary edge supervision strategy during training to
further improve boundary delineation.

Speciﬁcally, an auxiliary edge prediction head is attached
to the highest-resolution decoder feature before the ﬁnal
segmentation prediction layer. The edge head consists of a
depth-wise 3 × 3 convolution, batch normalization, ReLU
activation, and a 1 × 1 convolution, producing a one-channel
edge prediction map E. Given the ground-truth lesion mask
Y , the corresponding boundary target Ye is automatically
generated using a morphological gradient operation:

Ye = I [Dilate(Y ) −Erode(Y ) > 0]
(8)

where Dilate(·) and Erode(·) denote morphological dilation
and erosion operations using a 3 × 3 structuring element,
respectively, and I[·] is the indicator function.

Since boundary pixels occupy only a small proportion of
the image, we employ a weighted binary cross-entropy loss
combined with Dice loss for edge supervision:

Ledge = LW BCE(E, Ye) + LDice(E, Ye)
(9)

Accordingly, the overall training objective is formulated as:

Ltotal = loss + λeLedge
(10)

where loss denotes the original multi-scale segmentation
loss deﬁned in Section III-C, Ledge represents the auxiliary

IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Experimental Settings

1) Datasets: The proposed method is evaluated on four
benchmark datasets for skin lesion image segmentation, in-
cluding ISIC 2016, ISIC 2017, ISIC 2018, and PH2. These
benchmarks were constructed under the leadership of the
International Skin Imaging Collaboration (ISIC) to advance
the use of computer-aided diagnostic systems in skin diseases.

ISIC 2016: 1,279 dermoscopic images split into 788 train-
ing, 112 validation, and 379 test samples.

ISIC 2017: 2,150 images partitioned into training (1,290),
validation (215), and testing (645) dataset.

ISIC 2018: consists of 2,594, 100, and 1,000 training,
validation, and test images.

PH2: contains 200 skin lesion images. These images have
been randomly distributed into three groups, including 140,
20, 40 images for training, validation, and testing.
To further validate our model’s generalization and ro-
bustness, we also conducted experiments on datasets for
polyp segmentation, breast cancer segmentation, and cell nu-
clei/structure segmentation.

Polyp segmentation: Five distinct polyp segmentation
datasets are employed in our work, namely Kvasir [50]
(comprising 1,000 images), ClinicDB [51] (with 612 images),
ColonDB [52] (containing 379 images), ETIS [52] (consisting
of 196 images), and BKAI [53] (featuring 1,000 images).
These datasets are sourced from various imaging centers
and clinics, resulting in notable diversity in terms of image
characteristics, as well as the size and shape of the polyps
included.

Breast cancer segmentation: For the task of breast cancer
segmentation, the BUSI [54] dataset is utilized. In line with
the setup described in [55], we select 647 images from this
dataset, which includes 437 benign cases and 210 malignant
cases.

Cell nuclei/structure segmentation: To conduct cell nu-
clei/structure segmentation, we adopt two biological imaging
datasets: DSB18 [56] (which provides 670 images) and EM
[57] (offering 30 images).

For all datasets including ClinicDB, Kvasir, ColonDB,
ETIS, BKAI, DSB18, EM, and BUSI, a train-val-test split
ratio of 80:10:10 is applied.

2) Implementation Details: Our LMDAU-Net is imple-
mented with PyTorch and experimented on an Nvidia A40
GPU. The resolution of all images is standardized to 256×256.
Optimization employs the AdamW algorithm with a batch
size of 8 and an initial learning rate of 1 × 10−4, which is
reduced by a factor of 0.1 every 50 epochs. Training epochs
are conﬁgured as 150 (ISIC 2016), 200 (ISIC 2017/2018), and
50 (PH2) to accommodate dataset sizes.

For data augmentation, we apply random rotation (angle:
20◦–80◦) and random rotation-ﬂip (90° rotations + horizon-
tal/vertical ﬂips) during training, ensuring robustness to geo-
metric variations in skin lesions. Additionally, input images are
normalized using dataset-wide mean and standard deviation,
followed by min-max scaling to [0, 1] for consistent intensity
distribution.

The model is trained with a combined loss function:
BceDiceLoss, which balances binary cross-entropy and Dice
coefﬁcient. We use a ﬁxed input size of 256 × 256, 3 input
channels, and set the number of workers to 4. All results are
reported as mean ± standard deviation over ﬁve independent
runs with different random seeds. A ﬁxed set of seeds was
used for reproducibility. Model checkpoints are saved every
100 epochs, and validation is performed every 30 epochs.
For practical deployment evaluation, inference efﬁciency
was measured on the same NVIDIA A40 GPU with an
input resolution of 256 × 256 and a batch size of 1. The
inference time was measured per image under the same testing
environment, and the corresponding frames per second (FPS)
was calculated accordingly. Peak GPU memory was recorded
during inference using the maximum allocated GPU memory.
All models were evaluated using the same hardware and input
settings for a fair comparison.

C. Ablation Study

Unless otherwise speciﬁed, the ablation studies were con-
ducted on the ISIC 2018 dataset. The comparisons of alterna-
tive global modeling branches, practical inference efﬁciency,
and auxiliary edge supervision (AES) were performed on
the ISIC 2017 dataset under the same training and testing
protocol as the main comparison experiments to ensure a fair
evaluation.

1) Lightweight Local-global Learning Module: To assess
the effect of the Lightweight Local-global Learning Module,
we perform ablation experiments by removing or modify-
ing its core sub-components: a CNN branch and a Mamba
branch. As shown in Table VIItable.7, removing the entire
module causes obvious declines across all metrics, proving
the necessity of joint local and global feature modeling for
skin lesion segmentation. When only the convolutional branch
is discarded, the model retains competitive overall perfor-
mance, which indicates local CNN features form the basic
segmentation foundation. Incorporating the Mamba branch

further strengthens global context modeling and boosts ﬁnal
segmentation accuracy.

2) Multi-branch Mamba Extraction Block: We veriﬁed the

5)
Alternative Global Operator and Practical Inference
Efﬁciency: To examine whether the performance of LLM is
attributable to the Mamba branch rather than merely the use
of a lightweight global modeling operator, we constructed a
Performer-based linear-attention variant, termed LMDAU-Net
w/ Performer. Speciﬁcally, each Mamba-based VM block in
the global branch was replaced by a Performer-based linear
attention block [48], while the CNN branch, channel-splitting
strategy, AIF decoder, feature dimensions, training settings,
and input resolution were kept unchanged. This controlled
replacement enables a direct comparison between Mamba and
an alternative efﬁcient global modeling mechanism. As shown
in Table XItable.11, LMDAU-Net achieves a more favorable
accuracy-efﬁciency trade-off than the linear-attention variant.
Speciﬁcally, replacing the Mamba branch with linear attention
reduces Dice from 91.29% to 91.12% and IoU from 83.97% to
83.58%. Meanwhile, the original LMDAU-Net requires fewer
parameters and GFLOPs, and achieves lower inference latency
and peak GPU memory usage. Compared with the linear-
attention variant, LMDAU-Net reduces the inference time from
65.20 ms/image to 58.48 ms/image and the peak GPU memory
from 286.40 MiB to 237.80 MiB, while improving the FPS
from 15.34 to 17.10. Moreover, LMDAU-Net achieves the
lowest computational cost and memory consumption among
the evaluated models, while obtaining the highest Dice and
IoU scores. These results suggest that, under the same archi-
tecture and experimental protocol, the Mamba-based global
branch provides an effective balance between long-range fea-
ture modeling, segmentation accuracy, and practical inference
efﬁciency.

6) Effect of Auxiliary Edge Supervision: We investigate the
impact of auxiliary edge supervision (AES) on the overall seg-
mentation performance. As shown in Table XIItable.12, row
2 demonstrates that introducing AES improves the boundary-

related metrics compared with the original LMDAU-Net in
row 1. Speciﬁcally, BF1@2 increases from 86.52% to 88.69%,
while HD95 decreases from 12.35 px to 10.82 px. Meanwhile,
Dice and IoU remain comparable, changing from 91.29% and
83.97% to 91.35% and 83.99%, respectively. These results
indicate that AES effectively improves the boundary delin-
eation ability of LMDAU-Net without sacriﬁcing region-level
segmentation performance. Since the auxiliary edge branch
is only used during training, it does not introduce additional
computational cost during inference.

V. DISCUSSION
Accurate and efﬁcient skin lesion segmentation is critical
in clinical dermatology and oncology, as early detection and
precise delineation of lesions signiﬁcantly impact patient prog-
nosis. Moreover, accurate lesion boundary delineation can aid
dermatologists in preoperative assessments, may support lesion
delineation during clinical image review and preoperative
assessment.

Our LMDAU-Net performs better over various state-of-
the-art skin lesion segmentation models. Speciﬁcally, our
model achieves the highest Intersection over Union (IoU) and
Dice coefﬁcients across multiple datasets, demonstrating its
robustness in segmenting complex lesion shapes. Compared
to Transformer-based models like TransUNet and TransFuse,
our approach maintains comparable global feature extraction
capabilities while signiﬁcantly reducing computational over-
head. Unlike CNN-based methods that struggle with long-
range dependencies, LMDAU-Net effectively balances local
ﬁne-grained details and global contextual information using
its dual-branch feature extraction strategy. Furthermore, a key
advantage of LMDAU-Net is its ability to maintain high
accuracy while using lower parameters, which makes it well-
suited for real-world clinical applications.

Despite its promising performance, LMDAU-Net has sev-
eral limitations to address in future work. First, while it
cuts computation costs versus Transformer-based methods,
it still demands more resources than lightweight traditional
CNNs. Second, our model depends on fully annotated data for
supervised training. Third, its segmentation accuracy drops on
low-quality dermoscopic images with heavy hair occlusion,
reﬂections, low lesion-skin contrast or indistinct boundaries.
Besides, training hyperparameters (input resolution, learning
rate schedule, loss weights) were ﬁxed here and may affect
model behavior. Lastly, LMDAU-Net only serves as an aux-
iliary segmentation tool instead of an independent diagnostic
system; all predictions need clinician review, and issues in-
cluding data privacy, model bias and clinical responsibility
should be resolved before widespread clinical use. For future
research, we aim to lower computational overhead without
sacriﬁcing segmentation performance, adopt semi-supervised
learning to boost generalization on unlabeled samples, and
conduct validation on larger multi-center clinical datasets
covering various image qualities and acquisition setups.
